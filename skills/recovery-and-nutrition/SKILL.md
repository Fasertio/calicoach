# Recovery and Nutrition

Recovery is where adaptation happens. This skill covers the practical training
side of it and knows exactly where its scope ends.

**Announce it:** "Using recovery-and-nutrition to \<purpose\>."

## Scope — read this first

**In scope:** general, practical guidance on sleep, training-day food timing,
protein and energy sufficiency for training, hydration, stress and deload
decisions.

**Out of scope — refer:**
- Any eating-disorder history or current disordered-eating signals. Stop
  nutrition guidance entirely, say so kindly and plainly, and suggest a
  physician or a registered dietitian with eating-disorder experience.
- Medical nutrition therapy — diabetes, kidney disease, coeliac, IBD, pregnancy,
  hypertension requiring dietary management.
- Supplement protocols beyond ordinary food, and anything performance-enhancing.
- Extreme or rapid weight-loss requests, or making a weight class fast.
- Under-16 body-composition goals.

Say the boundary once, offer what you can do, and continue coaching training.

## Sleep — the highest-leverage variable

| Target | Detail |
|---|---|
| Duration | 7–9 h for most training adults |
| Consistency | same sleep and wake time ±1 h beats total hours alone |
| The training consequence | chronic sleep under ~6 h degrades strength expression, increases injury rates, and blunts adaptation |

**Practical prescription** (offer these; do not lecture):
- Fixed wake time, even at weekends.
- Last hard training session ending 3+ hours before bed where possible.
- Caffeine cut-off 8–10 h before bed.
- Dark, cool room; screens down 30 min before.
- If sleep is chronically short and cannot be fixed, **reduce training volume**
  rather than pretending the athlete will recover from it. That is the coaching
  decision, and it is not a moral one.

**Programming rule:** a night under 5 h means no maximal attempts, no new
leverage step, no true failure. Written into every program.

## Energy availability

The most common nutrition problem in this sport is not eating enough to support
training — and it is often accidental.

**Signals to watch for:** performance declining without a volume increase,
persistent fatigue, frequent illness, poor sleep, injuries that will not settle,
loss of menstrual regularity, low mood and irritability, feeling cold.

If several appear together, raise it plainly and without alarm, suggest a
conversation with a physician or dietitian, and reduce training volume in the
meantime. Do not attempt to manage relative energy deficiency yourself.

---

## Protein

| Goal | Practical range |
|---|---|
| General training | 1.4–1.8 g per kg bodyweight per day |
| Muscle gain | 1.6–2.2 g/kg |
| Fat loss while preserving muscle | 1.8–2.4 g/kg |

Distribution matters less than the total, but 3–5 servings of 0.3–0.4 g/kg across
the day is a reasonable practical default. Plant-based athletes should aim for
the upper end and use varied sources.

---

## Energy and body composition

| Goal | Approach |
|---|---|
| Maintain | eat to hold bodyweight stable across 2–3 weeks |
| Gain muscle | small surplus, ~0.25–0.5% bodyweight per week |
| Lose fat | modest deficit, 0.5–0.75% bodyweight per week; expect some performance cost |
| Aggressive loss | up to 1%/week, 8–12 weeks maximum, accept the performance cost, and only in an athlete with no eating-disorder history |

**Relevant to calisthenics specifically:** straight-arm skills scale poorly with
mass. An athlete chasing a planche or a front lever at a high bodyweight faces a
genuinely harder task. State it once as physics, never as a comment on their
body, and let them decide whether a body-composition phase precedes the skill
phase.

## Around training

| Timing | Practical guidance |
|---|---|
| 2–3 h before | a normal meal with carbohydrate and protein |
| 30–60 min before | if needed, something small and easily digested |
| During | water; for sessions over ~90 min or in heat, add electrolytes and some carbohydrate |
| After | a meal with protein and carbohydrate within a few hours — the "anabolic window" is much wider than commonly claimed |

Hydration: pale-yellow urine is a good enough practical marker. Thirst is a
reasonable guide for most training; heat, long sessions and heavy sweating need
deliberate intake.

## Stress and life load

Training stress and life stress draw on the same recovery budget. A high-stress
period is a reason to reduce volume, not a reason for the athlete to feel they
have failed.

**Practical rule:** when work or life stress is sustained at 8+/10, cut weekly
volume by 20–30% and keep intensity and frequency. Frequency preserves the habit;
volume is what costs recovery.

## Recovery methods — what is actually worth prescribing

| Method | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Sleep | by far the most effective. Everything else is a rounding error next to it |
| Adequate energy and protein | second most effective |
| Easy movement (walking, easy cycling) | genuinely helpful, low cost |
| Deloads | necessary, and the main structural tool you control |
| Massage, foam rolling | feels good, transient effects; fine if the athlete enjoys it, not a substitute for the above |
| Stretching | useful for range goals; does not prevent soreness or injury |
| Cold water immersion | may blunt hypertrophy adaptations if used routinely after strength training; use sparingly, and not right after the sessions that matter most |
| Sauna / heat | pleasant, some cardiovascular evidence, minor for recovery |
| Compression garments, cupping, percussive guns | mild subjective effects at best |
| Supplements | creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day) has the strongest evidence base for strength training; caffeine works for performance. Beyond that, food first. Anything else is out of scope |

Be honest about this table. Athletes spend money and time on the bottom half
while under-sleeping.

## Monitoring recovery

Track four simple things, weekly:

1. Sleep hours and quality.
2. Resting heart rate on waking (optional, but useful if they have a watch).
3. Motivation / mood 0–10.
4. Session performance versus plan.

Two of four degrading for a week is a deload signal. Do not need all four.

## Definition of done

- [ ] Scope boundary respected; referred where required
- [ ] Sleep addressed before anything else
- [ ] Energy-availability signals checked when performance is declining
- [ ] Protein and energy guidance given as practical ranges, not a diet plan
- [ ] Recovery methods ranked honestly
- [ ] Any life-stress load translated into a concrete volume adjustment
- [ ] Nothing prescribed that belongs to a dietitian or a physician
