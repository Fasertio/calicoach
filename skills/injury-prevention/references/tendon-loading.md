# Tendon Loading and Load Management

How to load an irritable tendon, and how to keep a healthy one healthy. This is
a **training framework** applied to a training problem — not a treatment plan.
Anything with red flags, or anything that does not improve over 4–6 weeks, goes
to a clinician.

---

## Why tendons dictate calisthenics programming

Muscle adapts in 3–8 weeks. Tendon and ligament adapt in 8–26 weeks. In a sport
built on straight-arm holds and long levers, the athlete's strength routinely
outruns their connective tissue by a wide margin — and the gap is where injuries
live.

Practical consequences:

- Straight-arm volume climbs at ≤10% per week even when everything feels easy.
- Strength returning after a layoff does **not** mean tolerance returned.
- Skill progress that feels "too easy for two weeks" is usually the tendon's
  loading window closing behind you.

---

## The graded loading progression

The widely used framework for irritable tendons moves through four stages. Each
stage is entered only when the previous one is tolerated. This structure is
associated with Cook, Purdam, Silbernagel and colleagues in the tendinopathy
literature — cite the approach, not a specific page.

### Stage 1 — Isometric loading (pain relief and tolerance)
- **Prescription:** 5 x 30–45 s holds at ~60–70% of maximal effort,
  2–3 minutes rest, 1–2x daily.
- **Position:** mid-range, not end-range.
- **Target pain:** ≤3/10 during, settled within 24 h.
- **Duration:** typically 1–2 weeks, or until pain during daily activity settles.

Examples: elbow flexor hold at 90°; wall sit or Spanish squat for the patellar
tendon; single-leg calf-raise hold at mid-range for the Achilles; isometric
shoulder external rotation hold.

### Stage 2 — Heavy slow resistance (the main event)
- **Prescription:** 3–4 sets x 6–8 reps, **3 s eccentric + 3 s concentric**,
  heavy load, 3x per week on non-consecutive days.
- **Target pain:** ≤3/10 during, settled within 24 h, no worse the next morning.
- **Duration:** 6–12 weeks. This is the stage that changes the tissue.

Examples: slow eccentric wrist curls; slow heavy curls for the distal biceps;
slow heavy leg press or Spanish squat for the patellar tendon; slow heavy calf
raises for the Achilles.

### Stage 3 — Energy storage
- Reintroduce faster, springier loading: light plyometrics, faster tempos,
  dynamic skill entries.
- **Only** when stage 2 is comfortable at a good load, and only progressing one
  variable at a time.

### Stage 4 — Return to the sport-specific position
- Reintroduce the actual skill (planche lean, lever hold, jumping, muscle-up
  transition) at **60% of the previous volume**, rebuilding over 3–4 weeks.

---

## The 24-hour rule

The decision rule that runs the whole framework:

> Pain during loading up to **3/10 is acceptable** if it settles to baseline
> within 24 hours and the next session is no worse.

If pain exceeds 4/10, or is worse the next morning, or is climbing week over
week — reduce the load by 20–30% and stay at that stage longer.

Record a daily 0–10 score for the affected structure. Trends beat single days.

## Region-specific starting points

| Region | Stage 1 isometric | Stage 2 heavy slow |
|---|---|---|
| **Distal biceps / elbow flexors** | elbow flexor hold at 90°, 5 x 30–45 s | slow eccentric curls / straight-arm pulldown, 3–4 x 6–8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Medial elbow (flexor origin)** | wrist flexion isometric, 5 x 30 s | wrist curls, 3–4 x 8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Lateral elbow (extensor origin)** | wrist extension isometric, 5 x 30 s | reverse wrist curls / flexbar eccentrics, 3–4 x 8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Patellar tendon** | Spanish squat or wall sit, 5 x 45 s | slow leg press or squat to a pain-free depth, 4 x 6–8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Achilles** | single-leg calf-raise hold, 5 x 45 s | heavy slow calf raises, straight and bent knee, 4 x 6–8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Rotator cuff / shoulder** | isometric external rotation, 5 x 30 s | slow cable/dumbbell external rotation and rows, 3–4 x 8 @ 3-0-3 |
| **Hamstring (proximal)** | isometric bridge hold, 5 x 30 s | slow RDL / Nordic regression, 3 x 6 @ 3-0-3 |

## What to do with the rest of the program

**Never stop training.** Remove the provoking load, keep everything else:

| Affected | Removed | Kept and often increased |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow (straight-arm) | planche, levers, one-arm, straight-arm pulldown at load | legs, core, pressing, moderate bent-arm pulling, all prehab |
| Shoulder | overhead, dips, deep pressing | pulling in the pain-free range, legs, core, cuff and scapular work |
| Patellar tendon | jumping, deep loaded flexion, pistols | upper body in full, hinge, isometric quad work, calf |
| Achilles | running, jumping, fast calf | upper body, hinge, isometric calf |
| Lumbar | loaded flexion, heavy hinge | upper body, isometric core, hip work in the pain-free range |

An athlete whose program disappears because one joint hurts loses fitness,
confidence and adherence. Rebuild the week around what is safe, same day.

## Load management guardrails (recap)

Two of these are now arithmetic rather than advice: `calicoach check` computes
the week-over-week climb of every straight-arm and skill-TUT row in the volume
budget, compounded across the weeks the columns actually span, and fails a block
that exceeds the cap. It also fails a progression plan that advances leverage
more often than one step per two weeks. A rule that only prose states is a rule
that gets broken on the week the athlete feels strong — which is precisely the
week it exists for.

| Guardrail | Number |
|---|---|
| Weekly hard-set increase | ≤ 10% |
| Weekly straight-arm TUT increase | ≤ 10% |
| Leverage advance | ≤ 1 per 2–3 weeks per skill |
| Week of a leverage advance | volume −30% |
| Deload | every 4–6 weeks or on signal |
| After 2–4 weeks off | restart at 60% volume |
| After 1–3 months off | 50% volume, regress leverage one step |
| After any elbow symptom | straight-arm work → zero for ≥1 week, restart at 60% |
| New skills introduced | one at a time, 4+ weeks apart |

## Acute injuries — what training does and does not do

For a fresh acute injury (a strain, a sprain, a pop), the coaching job is:
refer, then keep training everything else, then reintroduce gradually under
whatever the clinician allows. Do not prescribe a rehabilitation protocol; do
prescribe safe training for the rest of the body, and do help the athlete follow
their clinician's plan without adding load behind their back.

The old "rest until it stops hurting" approach has been superseded — modern
sports medicine favours early, graded, protected loading. Say that if the athlete
asks, and then point them at their clinician for the specifics.
