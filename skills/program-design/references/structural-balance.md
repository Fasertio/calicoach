# Structural Balance

The rule that decides whether a program builds an athlete or builds an injury.
Everything here is applied by the balance audit at the bottom, and enforced by
`npx calicoach check`.

Sources: `[OG2]` ch. 4 and ch. 9; `[BSTA]` ch. 1 and ch. 11. Where they differ,
both positions are given.

---

## 1. Why bodyweight training skews toward pushing

Gravity gives you pressing for free: lower the body, push it back up. Squats,
lunges, push-ups, dips, handstand push-ups — all available with no equipment at
all. **Pulling requires something to hang from.** A bar, rings, a suspension
trainer, or a sturdy table.

The consequence is structural: an athlete training with what is to hand will
accumulate pressing volume and neglect pulling, and the neglected muscles are
specifically the ones that hold the shoulder together — **scapular retractors,
posterior deltoids and external rotators** `[OG2]`. Those three are trained
almost entirely by horizontal pulling, which is the pattern most often missing.

So the bias is not a preference. It is a correction for a known asymmetry in the
tool, and it is why this framework requires pull volume ≥ push volume.

---

## 2. Classifying an exercise — the mechanical test

Anatomical classification confuses people, especially for isometrics. Use the
mechanical definition instead `[OG2]`:

> **Pull** — the hands and the body's centre of mass move *toward* each other.
> **Push** — the hands and the centre of mass move *away* from each other.

This resolves every case cleanly, including the static skills:

| Skill | Class | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Front lever | **pull** | hands pull down toward the body's line |
| Back lever | **pull** | arms pull toward the centre of mass throughout |
| Iron cross | **pull** | arms adducting toward the body |
| Planche | **push** | hands push down and away to hold the body up |
| Inverted cross | **push** | |
| Maltese, victorian | **both** | the borderline — full-body tension, pushing and pulling muscles firing together |

Then split each into vertical and horizontal:

| Quadrant | Contains |
|---|---|
| **Vertical push** | handstand, handstand push-up, overhead press, dips |
| **Horizontal push** | push-up variations, planche variations, bench press |
| **Vertical pull** | pull-ups, inverted pull-ups, iron cross |
| **Horizontal pull** | all rowing variations, front lever, back lever |

Note where the levers land: **front lever and back lever are horizontal
*pulling*.** A program that treats them as "core work" and then adds no rows has
no horizontal pulling at all.

## 3. The six balance axes

`[OG2]` uses push/pull; `[BSTA]` extends it to the whole body. The full set:

| # | Axis | One side | Other side |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Upper horizontal | horizontal push | horizontal pull |
| 2 | Upper vertical | vertical push | vertical pull |
| 3 | Lower | knee-dominant | hip-dominant |
| 4 | Core plane | linear (sagittal) | lateral |
| 5 | Core rotation | — | rotary / anti-rotation |
| 6 | Limb | bilateral | unilateral |

**Axis 6 is the one most programs miss entirely.** Single-limb work is not a
variation of bilateral work — it recruits stabilisers that bilateral work never
asks for: the adductors and abductors firing together to keep a knee tracking
over the foot in a split squat, the rotary core resisting a one-arm push-up
`[BSTA]`. And as an athlete advances, bilateral bodyweight work stops being hard
enough; unilateral variations become the only way to keep progressing without
external load.

**Axis 4/5** matters for the same reason. Planks and hollow holds are linear;
side planks and carries are lateral; Pallof presses and windshield wipers are
rotary. Train all three, not three variations of the first.

### How strictly to balance

The two sources differ in emphasis, and the honest answer sits between them:

- `[OG2]`: keep push and pull balanced; add corrective work when an imbalance
  starts to develop — most often more horizontal pulling.
- `[BSTA]`: it is not mandatory to hit every pattern in every workout, nor to
  match set counts exactly between opposing patterns. What matters is not
  **skewing** the program toward one pattern.

**This framework's rule:** across the *week*, every axis has both sides
represented, and pull volume is at least equal to push volume. Within a single
session, balance is not required. A pattern deliberately left under its landmark
is written down with the reason and a date when it comes back.

## 4. Choosing the exercises — the minimum balanced skeleton

From `[OG2]` ch. 4, adapted:

1. **One horizontal push** and **one horizontal pull**.
2. **One vertical push (downward)** and **one vertical pull**.
   - For beginners, the handstand covers vertical pushing *upward* as skill
     work, so the strength slot should be a vertical push *downward* — dips.
   - **Dips before handstand push-ups** for most new athletes: dips are the
     upper-body squat, and they build the pressing base and muscle volume that
     everything else stands on. Handstands stay in the routine as skill work, so
     the overhead range is never neglected.
3. **At least two leg exercises**, one knee-dominant and one hip-dominant.
4. **Core across the three planes**, not three variations of one.
5. **At least one unilateral movement**, upper or lower.

Where the athlete's goals are unevenly distributed across the quadrants — three
pushing goals and no pulling goal, say — the missing quadrants get filled from
the exercise catalogue or from
[weight-room-integration](../../weight-room-integration/SKILL.md), not left
empty because nothing there is exciting.

## 5. Two structural pairings worth knowing

### Handstand ↔ L-sit → V-sit → manna
`[OG2]` recommends training these as a coupled pair. The handstand loads the
**overhead** end of shoulder range; the manna progression loads the **backward
extension** end. Training stability at both extremes reinforces strength through
everything between them, and the L-sit/manna line trains the scapular
retractors, posterior deltoids and external rotators in one series — the exact
three areas a pressing-heavy routine leaves behind.

If the manna progression is not a goal, the alternative is deliberate horizontal
pulling that emphasises driving the elbows back and squeezing the shoulder
blades together. That is more exercises for the same effect.

### Planche ↔ front lever
Opposing straight-arm skills, one push and one pull, at matched difficulty. They
pair well as antagonists (see §6) and they balance each other's shoulder
loading. They do **not** balance each other's *elbow* loading — both load the
elbow flexors at length, and the volumes add. Cap them together, not separately.

## 6. Antagonistic pairing — how to fit a balanced session into real time

The most common reason a program becomes unbalanced is time: the session runs
long, and the pulling and leg work at the bottom of the sheet gets dropped.

**Pair opposing patterns and halve the rest** `[OG2]` `[BSTA]`. Perform exercise
A, rest half the normal interval, perform its antagonist B, rest half again,
repeat. The working muscles still get their full recovery because the antagonist
set is their rest.

```
Planche isometric → 2.5 min → Front lever isometric → 2.5 min → repeat x3
Same work as 3 x 5-min-rest of each, in roughly half the wall-clock time.
```

Rules:

- Pair genuine antagonists: horizontal push with horizontal pull, vertical push
  with vertical pull, knee-dominant with hip-dominant.
- **Do not pair movements that share muscles.** Push-ups with handstand push-ups
  is not a pair, it is a superset `[BSTA]`.
- Pairs, not triplets. Three couplets beat two triplets — cycling through more
  exercises taxes the body more without buying anything `[OG2]`.
- Accept the trade: strength gains are slightly below fully-rested sets, but
  close, and a paired session that happens beats a full-rest session that does
  not.
- Do not confuse with **supersets** (same muscle group, no rest, hypertrophy) or
  **drop sets** (same exercise, reduced load at failure).

**When a session does not fit the time budget, pair antagonists first. Cut an
exercise only when pairing is not enough** — and when you do cut, cut from the
pattern that is furthest above its landmark, never the warm-up or the prehab.

Frequency also buys balance: four 30-minute sessions fit more balanced work than
two 60-minute ones, and `[BSTA]` recommends choosing frequency over per-session
volume. **Whole-body routines are the default for bodyweight training** — the
athletes who get the most out of bodyweight work overwhelmingly train the whole
body each session rather than splitting it.

## 7. Exercise order

Two rules, and they can conflict. Resolve in this order:

1. **The goal goes first.** Whatever the block is for gets the freshest effort.
   Fatigue accumulates across a session: by roughly fifteen total sets an athlete
   is working at 90–95% of capacity, and the drop is invisible unless the order
   is varied `[OG2]`.
2. **Then largest to smallest.** The general rule of thumb: knee-dominant →
   hip-dominant → upper-body pull → upper-body press → core and small muscles
   `[BSTA]`. If power, strength and conditioning are all in one session, that is
   also the order.

For this framework's session template, that means: warm-up → **skill work** (the
goal, and it is CNS-demanding) → primary strength → secondary → prehab →
cool-down. If the block's goal is a strength lift rather than a skill, that lift
moves to the top.

Changing the order mid-block is allowed and sometimes useful — it is a cheap way
to accelerate whichever exercise you move up.

## 8. Warning signs of imbalance

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| **New** clicking, popping or cracking in a joint that never did it before | uneven muscle development around that joint — audit the balance before anything else `[OG2]` |
| Shoulders rounding forward, tight chest | pressing volume without matching horizontal pulling `[BSTA]` |
| Anterior shoulder pain in pressing and support positions | anterior musculature outpacing the posterior shoulder |
| Knee pain with quad dominance, weak end-range hip extension | knee-dominant work without hip-dominant work `[BSTA]` |
| Strength plateau with no other explanation | the nervous system limiting output around a perceived instability `[OG2]` |
| One side visibly behind on every unilateral test | no unilateral work in the program |

Long-standing painless clicking is usually nothing. **Onset** of clicking, or
clicking that gets louder or arrives with pain, during a program that leans one
way, is a finding.

## 9. The balance audit — run this on every program

Before delivering, check every line. `npx calicoach check` runs the mechanical
parts of it.

- [ ] **Axis 1** — horizontal push and horizontal pull both present
- [ ] **Axis 2** — vertical push and vertical pull both present
- [ ] **Axis 3** — knee-dominant and hip-dominant both present
- [ ] **Axis 4** — core trained in more than one plane
- [ ] **Axis 6** — at least one unilateral movement
- [ ] **Pull ≥ push** in weekly hard sets
- [ ] Horizontal pulling specifically present — not just vertical pulling
- [ ] Levers, if programmed, counted as **pulling**, not as core
- [ ] Any axis left one-sided is written down with the reason and a return date
- [ ] Constraints checked: where a constraint removes one side of an axis, the
      substitution restores that side rather than dropping it
- [ ] Skills that share tissue (planche + front lever, any two straight-arm
      skills) have a **combined** volume cap, not two separate ones

## 10. What balance is not

It is not symmetry for its own sake, and it is not a reason to add exercises. A
program with eleven exercises balanced across six axes and no time to perform
them is worse than seven exercises that cover the axes and get done. Balance is
achieved by **choosing the right seven**, and by pairing antagonists so they fit
— not by lengthening the list.
