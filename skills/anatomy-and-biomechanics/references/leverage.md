# Leverage and Mechanics

Why a tuck is easier than a straddle, and a straddle easier than a full lay. This
is the mathematics behind every skill progression, and it is worth explaining to
athletes — it converts "I'm too weak" into "the lever is too long".

---

## The core idea: torque

For a static hold, the demand at a joint is the **torque** it must resist:

```
Torque = Force x Moment arm
```

In a lever or planche, the force is the athlete's weight acting at their centre
of mass, and the **moment arm is the horizontal distance from the shoulder to the
centre of mass**.

Two consequences:

1. **Difficulty scales with that horizontal distance.** Halving it roughly halves
   the torque.
2. **It is horizontal distance that matters, not total body length.** A tuck
   brings the mass close to the shoulder; the body has not become lighter, the
   lever has become shorter.

---

## Approximate difficulty of the lever progressions

Relative shoulder torque, taking a full front lever as 100%. These are estimates
for orientation, not measurements — individual proportions move them.

| Rung | Approx. relative torque |
|---|---|
| Tuck | 25–35% |
| Advanced tuck | 40–50% |
| One leg out | 55–70% |
| Straddle (wide) | 65–80% |
| Straddle (narrow) | 80–90% |
| Full lay | 100% |

**Why the ladder has the steps it has:** the jump from advanced tuck to straddle
is roughly a 20–30% increase in torque. That is a big jump — equivalent to adding
20–30% to a barbell lift in one session. Hence the rule that volume drops 30% in
the week of a leverage advance.

**Why straddle width is a real variable:** widening the straddle moves mass
laterally and closer to the shoulder axis, shortening the effective moment arm.
Narrowing it is a genuine progression step, and it lets you make jumps far
smaller than a full rung.

---

## Body proportions

- **Longer limbs and a longer torso lengthen the moment arm.** A taller athlete
  is doing genuinely harder work in the same position.
- **Heavier athletes face proportionally more torque** at every rung. Strength
  does not scale linearly with mass — cross-sectional area scales roughly with
  the square of a linear dimension while mass scales with the cube, which is why
  lighter athletes dominate straight-arm skills.

Explain this once, as physics, when an athlete is comparing themselves to someone
on the internet. Then get back to the ladder.

## Leverage in other exercises

| Exercise | The leverage variable | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Push-up | angle of the body relative to the floor | incline (easier) → flat → decline (harder), in ~10° steps |
| Ring row | angle of the body | walk the feet in or out, or elevate the feet |
| Planche lean | horizontal distance from shoulder to wrist | measure in cm; the cleanest progression variable in the sport |
| Pistol squat | position of the free leg and any counterweight | a counterweight held forward shifts the centre of mass and reduces the demand |
| Nordic curl | how far you descend before catching | range is the load |
| Ab wheel | how far the arms extend | knees → toes, and range within each |
| Human flag | body position and hand spacing | tuck → one leg → straddle → full; wider hands change the demand distribution |
| Hollow hold | how far the legs are from the floor | lower legs = longer lever = harder |
| L-sit | parallettes vs floor | shorter effective arms on the floor make it harder |

## Moment arms at the joint, not the whole body

Within an exercise, joint-level moment arms explain the sticking points:

- **Pull-up:** the moment arm at the elbow is longest around 90° of elbow flexion
  — the classic sticking point in the middle of the pull.
- **Squat:** the moment arm at the hip is longest near parallel, which is why
  that is where a squat is missed.
- **Dip:** the moment arm at the shoulder grows as you descend, which is why the
  bottom is both the hardest and the riskiest part.
- **Bench press:** the moment arm at the shoulder grows with a wider grip, which
  is why wide-grip pressing loads the shoulder more.

Coaching consequence: pause reps at the sticking point train exactly where the
lift fails, and partial-range work above it does not.

## Open chain vs closed chain

| | Open chain | Closed chain |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | the distal segment moves freely | the distal segment is fixed against a surface |
| Examples | leg extension, curl, lat pulldown | squat, push-up, pull-up, planche |
| Characteristics | easier to isolate, easier to load precisely | more stabiliser demand, more transferable to skills |

Calisthenics is almost entirely closed chain. Open-chain work from the weight
room is how you isolate a lagging muscle or load a pattern precisely — which is
exactly what [weight-room-integration](../../weight-room-integration/SKILL.md)
is for.

## Contraction types and where they matter

| Type | What it is | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Isometric | force without movement | holds; tendon loading; sticking-point work; the entire straight-arm skill category |
| Concentric | muscle shortening | the "up" of a rep; the weakest of the three |
| Eccentric | muscle lengthening under load | the strongest — an athlete can lower more than they can lift, which is why eccentric-only work builds a first pull-up |

**Practical rules:**

- Eccentric-only training is the fastest route to a first pull-up or dip, and to
  strength after a plateau. It also produces the most soreness — start at 3 x 3.
- Isometrics build tendon tolerance and are the entry stage for irritable tissue.
- Overcoming isometrics (pushing against an immovable object) are excellent for
  sticking points and produce very little fatigue.

---

## Stability as load

Rings, unstable surfaces and single-limb positions add demand that behaves like
extra load without being measurable as load.

Practical rule: when moving a movement **from bar to rings**, or from bilateral
to unilateral, reduce the volume by 20–30% for the first two weeks. The athlete
is not weaker; the task is harder.
