# Joint-by-Joint Reference

What each joint does, what usually limits it, and what loads it in calisthenics.

---

## Shoulder complex

Four articulations working together: glenohumeral, scapulothoracic,
acromioclavicular, sternoclavicular. Most "shoulder problems" in calisthenics are
scapular control problems wearing a glenohumeral costume.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation, horizontal ab/adduction, plus scapular elevation/depression, protraction/retraction, upward/downward rotation |
| **Typical usable range** | flexion ~180°, abduction ~180°, external rotation ~90° at 90° abduction, extension ~50–60° |
| **Usual limiter** | thoracic extension, lat and pec length, scapular upward rotation |
| **Loaded hard by** | dips (extension), planche (flexion under lean), German hang and back lever (extreme extension), handstand (end-range flexion), muscle-up transition (extension under load), human flag (end-range abduction) |
| **Structures at risk** | rotator cuff and subacromial structures, long head of biceps in the bicipital groove, anterior capsule, labrum, AC joint |

**Scapulohumeral rhythm:** roughly 2° of glenohumeral motion per 1° of scapular
upward rotation through elevation. When the scapula does not upwardly rotate — a
stiff thoracic spine, a weak serratus or lower trapezius — the humerus runs out
of room and the athlete compensates with a shrug or a lumbar arch. That is why
overhead range gets screened before overhead pressing is programmed.

**Coaching consequence:** every program contains scapular work (retraction,
depression, protraction, upward rotation). Face pulls, prone Y raises, scapular
pull-ups and serratus work are not optional extras.

## Elbow

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension (humeroulnar), pronation/supination (radioulnar) |
| **Typical usable range** | 0–145° flexion, ~85° pronation, ~90° supination |
| **Usual limiter** | tissue tolerance, not range |
| **Loaded hard by** | **all straight-arm work** — planche, front and back lever, one-arm pull-up, straight-arm pulldown, false grip |
| **Structures at risk** | distal biceps tendon, brachialis, common flexor origin (medial), common extensor origin (lateral), ulnar nerve at the cubital tunnel |

**Why straight-arm work is different:** with the elbow locked, the elbow flexors
(biceps, brachialis) resist the extension torque isometrically at a long muscle
length, and the tendon takes a large share of the load. Muscle adapts in weeks;
tendon in months. That mismatch is the single most important programming fact in
calisthenics.

**Coaching consequence:** straight-arm volume caps, grip rotation, elbow prehab
every session, and zero tolerance for elbow symptoms during skill work.

## Wrist and hand

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, radial/ulnar deviation, plus finger and thumb function |
| **Typical usable range** | extension ~70–90°, flexion ~80°, ulnar deviation ~30°, radial ~20° |
| **Usual limiter** | extension range and weight-bearing tolerance |
| **Loaded hard by** | handstands, planche, floor pressing, false grip (extreme flexion), human flag |
| **Structures at risk** | TFCC (ulnar side), extensor tendons, carpal structures |

**Coaching consequence:** the wrist adapts to frequency more than intensity.
Short daily exposure, parallettes and push-up handles when symptomatic, and a
+10%/week ceiling on weight-bearing time.

---

## Thoracic spine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, rotation (the main rotational segment of the spine), lateral flexion |
| **Usual limiter** | extension, especially in desk-bound athletes |
| **Why it matters** | thoracic extension is a prerequisite for overhead range; without it the lumbar spine arches to compensate |

**Coaching consequence:** if the wall overhead reach screen fails, thoracic
extension work goes into the warm-up of every session, and overhead pressing is
substituted (landmine, incline) until it passes.

---

## Lumbar spine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, limited rotation (~5° per segment total) |
| **Design role** | stability, not mobility. Rotation belongs to the thoracic spine and the hips |
| **Loaded hard by** | levers and planche (anti-extension), hinges (anti-flexion under load), overhead work (anti-extension), carries (anti-lateral-flexion) |
| **Structures at risk** | discs (loaded flexion under load), facet joints (repeated end-range extension), pars interarticularis (repeated extension in young athletes) |

**Coaching consequence:** train the lumbar spine to resist movement. The hollow
body is the central skill. Loaded end-range flexion and repeated end-range
extension are both avoided, and pain with a directional preference gets
programmed away from that direction.

**Say honestly:** "core stability" as a treatment for back pain is far less
clearly supported than its popularity suggests, and pain correlates poorly with
imaging findings. Train the trunk because the skills demand it, not as a cure.

## Hip

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation |
| **Typical usable range** | flexion ~120° (more with external rotation), extension ~20°, abduction ~45°, rotation ~40° each way |
| **Usual limiter** | bony morphology (highly individual), then capsule and muscle length |
| **Loaded hard by** | squats, hinges, pistols, straddle positions, leg raises and compression work |
| **Structures at risk** | labrum (deep flexion with impingement morphology), adductor origins, hip flexor tendons, proximal hamstring |

**Individual variation is the rule.** Squat stance and depth are shaped by hip
anatomy that neither the athlete nor the coach can see. Coach to the position
that is pain-free and controlled for *this* hip, not to a universal ideal.

---

## Knee

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | flexion/extension, small rotation when flexed |
| **Loaded hard by** | squats, pistols, jumping, deep loaded flexion |
| **Structures at risk** | patellar tendon, patellofemoral joint, menisci, ACL/MCL (cutting and landing) |

**Knees over toes** is normal, necessary for deep squatting and pistols, and not
inherently harmful for a healthy knee. What matters is *load* and *rate of
increase*, not whether the knee passes the toe. Valgus collapse under load is the
fault worth correcting.

---

## Ankle and foot

| | |
|---|---|
| **Actions** | dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, inversion/eversion |
| **Typical usable range** | dorsiflexion ~10 cm knee-to-wall, plantarflexion ~50° |
| **Usual limiter** | dorsiflexion — soft tissue or bony anterior impingement |
| **Loaded hard by** | squats, pistols, jumping, running |
| **Structures at risk** | Achilles tendon, lateral ligaments, plantar fascia |

**Coaching consequence:** dorsiflexion limits squat depth and makes pistols
inaccessible. Heel elevation is a legitimate permanent solution, not only a
temporary fix.
