# Coaching Principles and Training Theory

The reasoning model behind every prescription. When a decision is not obvious,
come back here rather than defaulting to a popular template.

---

## 1. The hierarchy of decisions

Decide in this order. A lower item may never break a higher one.

1. **Safety / contraindications** — what this athlete must not do right now.
2. **Adherence** — what they will actually do, given their schedule and their
   temperament. The best program they skip is worthless.
3. **Specificity** — does the work resemble the goal in movement pattern, joint
   angle, contraction type and velocity?
4. **Overload** — is there a mechanism for the work to get harder over time?
5. **Fatigue management** — can they recover from it, week after week?
6. **Variation** — enough to keep tissue and mind fresh, little enough to keep
   the progression measurable.

If you ever feel tempted to add an exercise "because it's cool", it fails item 3
and 5 simultaneously. Cut it.

---

## 2. Specificity in calisthenics

Bodyweight skills are **strength expressed at a specific joint angle and a
specific lever length**. Training transfer falls off sharply as those diverge.

- A planche is a straight-arm, closed-chain, anterior-lean shoulder-flexion
  isometric with scapular protraction. Bench press does not build it. Pseudo
  planche push-ups, planche leans and tuck holds do.
- A front lever is a straight-arm scapular depression/extension isometric with a
  massive anti-extension core demand. Lat pulldowns contribute a little;
  straight-arm pulldowns, tuck lever holds and hollow-body work contribute a lot.
- A muscle-up is a high-velocity pull plus a transition. Training only slow
  pull-ups builds the pull and never the transition.

**Rule:** for a skill goal, at least one weekly exposure must be the skill itself
or its nearest technical regression, performed fresh, before fatigue.

## 3. Straight-arm vs bent-arm strength

This distinction is the single most common cause of elbow injury in
calisthenics.

- **Bent-arm strength** (pull-ups, dips, push-ups, rows) loads muscle primarily.
  It adapts fast — weeks.
- **Straight-arm strength** (planche, lever, maltese, iron cross) loads the
  **elbow and shoulder connective tissue** — biceps distal tendon, brachialis,
  common flexor origin, long head of biceps at the shoulder. It adapts slowly —
  months.

Consequences for programming:

- Never let straight-arm volume climb faster than ~10% per week, and cap
  progression jumps at one regression level per 2–3 weeks.
- Bent-arm and straight-arm work respond to different progression rates. Run them
  on separate progression clocks in the program.
- A beginner should own **8+ weeks of bent-arm base** and pain-free full-ROM
  scapular control before any planche or lever leaning work.
- Elbow warning signs (see [injury-prevention](../../injury-prevention/SKILL.md))
  always cut straight-arm volume first, not last.

## 4. Volume: landmarks, not dogma

Use weekly **hard sets per movement pattern** (sets taken within ~0–4 reps of
failure), not total sets.

| | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical pull | 4–8 | 8–14 | 12–20 |
| Horizontal pull | 4–8 | 8–14 | 12–20 |
| Vertical push | 3–6 | 6–12 | 10–18 |
| Horizontal push | 4–8 | 8–14 | 12–20 |
| Squat / knee-dominant | 4–8 | 8–14 | 12–20 |
| Hip hinge | 3–6 | 6–10 | 8–14 |
| Core / anti-extension | 4–8 | 6–12 | 8–15 |

Straight-arm isometric work is counted differently: use **total quality time
under tension per week**, in seconds, per skill.

| Skill stage | Weekly TUT (s) |
|---|---|
| Introduction (first 4 weeks) | 60–120 |
| Development | 120–240 |
| Peaking | 90–180, higher intensity, lower volume |

Quality time only. A shaking, collapsing 5 s hold is 0 s of quality.

**MEV / MAV / MRV framing.** Start a block near the minimum effective volume,
add roughly 1–2 sets per pattern per week while performance and recovery hold,
and treat the first week where reps drop, sleep degrades or joints ache as the
maximum recoverable volume signal — deload from there.

## 5. Intensity: RPE and RIR for bodyweight

Bodyweight load is not adjustable in 2.5 kg steps, so intensity is governed by
**leverage, tempo and reps in reserve**.

- **RIR (reps in reserve)** for rep work. Most working sets sit at RIR 2–3.
  Technical/skill work sits at RIR 3–5. Failure is a tool, not a habit — use it
  on isolation or machine work, rarely on compound bodyweight work, and never on
  straight-arm isometrics.
- **RPE for isometrics** — hold to the point where form is about to break, not to
  the point where it breaks. That is roughly RPE 8. Stop the set at the first
  loss of position (elbow bend, hip sag, scapular collapse).
- **Leverage as load.** Moving from tuck to advanced tuck to straddle is a step
  change in intensity that can exceed 20%. Treat every leverage change like a big
  jump on the bar: reduce volume in the week you make it.

## 6. Progression models

Pick one per exercise and state it explicitly in the program.

| Model | Use when | How it looks |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear reps** | beginner, bent-arm work | 3x6 -> 3x7 -> 3x8, then harder variation |
| **Double progression** | most rep work | work up to top of rep range in all sets, then progress the variation or add load |
| **Density** | conditioning, endurance goals | same work, less rest, week over week |
| **Leverage progression** | skills, straight-arm | tuck -> adv. tuck -> one-leg -> straddle -> full |
| **Load progression** | weighted calisthenics, weight room | add 1–2.5 kg when top of range is hit |
| **Time progression** | isometrics | 5x10 s -> 5x12 s -> 5x15 s, then next leverage |
| **Wave / undulating** | intermediate+, avoids stagnation | heavy / medium / light within the week |
| **Autoregulated** | inconsistent recovery, shift work | prescribe RIR and a rep range, let the day decide |

**Exit criterion.** Every progression needs a written rule for when to advance
*and* when to regress. "Advance when you hit 3x8 with clean form and RIR>=2 on
two consecutive sessions; regress if reps drop more than 20% for two sessions."

## 7. Fatigue, recovery and the tendon clock

Different tissues adapt on different clocks. Program to the slowest one that the
exercise loads.

| Tissue | Meaningful adaptation | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Neural / skill | days–2 weeks | early gains are learning, not tissue — do not chase them with volume |
| Muscle | 3–8 weeks | standard hypertrophy blocks |
| Tendon / ligament | 8–26 weeks | why straight-arm work must creep |
| Bone | 3–6 months | why sudden impact/plyo volume is risky |

**The 10% rule, applied honestly.** Weekly increases of total hard sets or total
isometric TUT above ~10% are where overuse injuries cluster. After any layoff of
2+ weeks, restart at ~60% of previous volume and rebuild over 2–3 weeks.

**Deloads.** Every 4–6 weeks, or sooner on signal. A deload keeps intensity and
cuts volume by 40–60%; it does not turn into a week of cardio. Signals to deload
early: performance drops 2 sessions running, resting HR up, sleep degraded,
persistent joint ache, motivation collapse, sudden clumsiness in skill work.

## 8. Session architecture

The order within a session is not cosmetic — it decides what actually improves.

1. **Raise / prepare** (8–12 min) — pulse raiser, joint prep for the joints the
   session will load, specific ramp-ups. See
   [injury-prevention](../../injury-prevention/SKILL.md).
2. **Skill / CNS-demanding work** (10–20 min) — handstand, lever, planche,
   muscle-up transitions. Fresh, low fatigue, RIR 3–5, stop before quality drops.
3. **Primary strength** (2–4 exercises) — heaviest compound pulls and pushes.
4. **Secondary / hypertrophy** (2–4 exercises) — accessory volume, often
   weight-room movements.
5. **Prehab / isolation** (2–3 exercises) — rotator cuff, scapular, elbow
   tendon-specific, hip and knee support work.
6. **Cool-down** (5 min) — breathing down, gentle mobility for the day's joints.

Never put skill work after a fatiguing pull block; you will train the compensation
pattern, not the skill.

## 9. Weekly structure by availability

| Days/week | Recommended split | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full body A / B | highest ROI per session; 6–10 hard sets per pattern per week |
| 3 | Full body A/B/C, or Push / Pull / Legs+Core | best default for most people |
| 4 | Upper / Lower x2, or Push / Pull / Legs / Skill | good for intermediates |
| 5 | PPL + Upper + Skill, or U/L/U/L/Skill | requires solid recovery habits |
| 6 | PPL x2 with one low-intensity day | advanced only, needs sleep and food dialled in |

Skill practice is not a "day". It is a daily-to-4x-weekly *short* exposure at the
start of sessions. Handstand especially: frequent and brief beats rare and long.

## 10. Periodisation for calisthenics

Use **block periodisation** with 3–6 week blocks and a clear primary aim.
Secondary qualities are maintained, not developed.

| Block type | Length | Primary aim | Typical content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation / GPP | 4–8 wk | tissue tolerance, technique, base volume | full ROM basics, high-rep, prehab-heavy |
| Hypertrophy | 4–6 wk | muscle cross-section | 6–15 reps, RIR 1–3, weight room integrated |
| Strength | 4–6 wk | max force, weighted calisthenics | 3–6 reps, RIR 2–3, longer rests |
| Skill / straight-arm | 4–6 wk | leverage progression | isometrics, low volume, high frequency, tight recovery |
| Peaking | 2–3 wk | express the skill | high intensity, low volume, extra rest |
| Deload / restoration | 1 wk | dissipate fatigue | 40–60% volume, intensity maintained |

A beginner does not need periodisation beyond "foundation, then keep adding". A
beginner *does* need a deload — schedule it anyway; it teaches the habit.

## 11. Weighted calisthenics guardrails

- Do not add external load to a movement until the athlete can perform **8–10
  clean bodyweight reps at full ROM** with control at both ends.
- Weighted dips are the most common source of shoulder and sternoclavicular
  complaints in this sport. Limit depth to the point where the humerus reaches
  roughly parallel with the torso line unless mobility is demonstrably excellent,
  and progress load slower than pull-ups.
- Weighted pull-ups: progress by ~2.5% bodyweight-equivalent per step, not by
  what is available on the rack.
- Rings add stability demand that behaves like extra load. Reduce volume when
  moving a movement from bar to rings.

## 12. Common programming errors to actively avoid

| Error | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Push:pull ratio favouring push | shoulder impingement, anterior tilt | program 1:1 to 1:1.5 in favour of pull |
| Skill work while fatigued | grooves compensations, high injury risk | skill goes first, always |
| Adding a new skill every block | nothing progresses | max 2 skill targets per block |
| No horizontal pull | scapular retractors underdeveloped, forward shoulder | at least 4 weekly hard sets of rows |
| Ignoring legs "because calisthenics" | asymmetric athlete, low work capacity, knee issues later | 2 knee-dominant + 1 hinge pattern weekly minimum |
| Progressing on a bad rep | ingrains fault, loads wrong tissue | reps that break the standard do not count |
| Jumping leverage on a good day | tendon load spike | leverage changes are planned, not improvised |
| Endless AMRAP conditioning | junk fatigue, masks strength progress | conditioning gets its own budget, capped |
| Static stretching before strength | transient force loss | dynamic prep before, static after |

---

## 13. What to write down, always

Any program you produce must state, explicitly:

- the **block aim** in one sentence,
- the **progression rule and exit criterion** for each exercise,
- the **regression** for each exercise and when to take it,
- the **contraindications** carried from `screening.md`,
- what to do on a **bad day** (the autoregulation rule),
- when the block is **reviewed** and what will be measured.

A program without these is a workout list, not coaching.
